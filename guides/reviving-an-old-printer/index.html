Reviving an old printer from storage: is it worth it?

Guide5 min readBy the Ink Comparison teamUpdated 24 Nov 2026

There's a printer in the loft, the garage or the back of a cupboard, and you need to print something. It worked when you put it away, so surely it just needs plugging in? Sometimes it does. Sometimes it's a lost cause. Here's how to tell which, without spending more than the printer is worth.

In short: a laser printer usually wakes up happily. An inkjet that's sat unused for a year or more will probably need new cartridges and some patience, and may not recover at all. Before you spend anything, check you can still buy cartridges for it and that your computer or phone can still talk to it.

Before you plug it in

  • Let it warm up. If it's been somewhere cold, like a garage or loft, give it a few hours indoors first. Canon warns that sudden temperature changes can cause condensation inside, and ink can freeze.
  • Check for leaks and mess. Look inside for dried ink, spilled toner or anything that's moved in transit. Clean up toner with a damp cloth and cold water, not a vacuum cleaner.
  • Hold off on the internet. If you use compatible cartridges, check the printer's firmware update settings before connecting it to Wi-Fi. An old printer that hasn't been online for years may install a backlog of updates, and on some HP printers an update can stop compatible cartridges working. See How to stop firmware updates blocking your cartridges.

Will the old cartridges still work?

It depends on how long they've been sitting there, and whether they're in the printer or still sealed.

  • Cartridges left in the printer: Epson recommends using a cartridge within six months of opening, and Canon says the same once one is installed. After a year or more in a printer that hasn't been used, expect them to be dried out or clogged. They may still work after a few cleaning cycles, but don't count on it.
  • Sealed spares: these keep much better. Check the date on the box. HP says a sealed cartridge typically lasts 18 to 24 months, and a few months past the date is often fine for everyday printing. There's more in How long do ink cartridges last?
  • "Expired" warnings: some HP printers warn when a cartridge passes its date. Pressing OK usually lets you carry on, but owners report that some printers refuse a cartridge outright. HP's warranty doesn't cover damage caused by expired cartridges.
  • Toner: keeps much better than ink, because it's a dry powder. Canon advises storing it between 0°C and 35°C, out of direct sunlight, in its sealed bag. Before you refit a toner cartridge, Canon says to gently shake it five or six times to spread the toner evenly.

Waking up an inkjet

How easily an inkjet recovers depends on where its print head is. If the head is part of the cartridge, as on many cheaper HP and Canon printers, a fresh cartridge brings a fresh head, so a new set of cartridges often solves everything. If the head is built into the printer, as on most Epson and Brother models, dried ink in the head is harder to shift.

  1. Fit the cartridges (old ones first, if they're not obviously empty) and let the printer finish its start-up routine.
  2. Print a nozzle check or test page from the printer's maintenance menu.
  3. If there are gaps or streaks, run a cleaning cycle, then check again. Do this two or three times at most, as each cycle uses ink.
  4. Still patchy? Leave it overnight, then try again. Epson suggests replacing a cartridge that's more than six months old if cleaning and aligning the head don't help.

If that doesn't work, our How to unclog a printer guide goes further, including when to stop.

Waking up a laser printer

Lasers cope with storage much better, because there's no ink to dry out. Gently shake the toner cartridge as above, fit it, and print a few test pages. If paper won't feed, the rubber pickup rollers may have dried out or picked up dust. Your printer's manual will explain how to clean them, usually with a lint-free cloth.

Can you still buy cartridges for it?

Makers don't keep making cartridges for old printers forever. HP says it doesn't maintain older cartridge families indefinitely, and that availability varies by cartridge and region. Before you spend time reviving a printer, check you can still buy ink or toner for it, and what it costs.

Search for your printer at the top of this page, or browse our printer list. We list original and compatible cartridges from UK shops, so you can see at a glance whether cartridges are still sold for your model, and what a full set costs. If only one or two shops still stock them, or a set costs as much as a new printer, that's your answer.

Will it work with my computer or phone?

This is often what really finishes an old printer off.

  • Windows: Microsoft is winding down support for printer makers' own drivers. Since January 2026 no new ones are published to Windows Update, and from July 2026 Windows prefers its own built-in printer driver. Existing drivers can still be installed, and makers can still offer them on their own websites, but some very old printers may lose features such as ink level reporting, or not work at all.
  • Phones and Macs: many newer printers support AirPrint or Mopria, which work without extra software. Older ones may not.
  • Apps: the makers' apps don't support every old model. HP, for example, has replaced HP Smart with a new HP app, and says not all its printers work with it.
  • Wi-Fi: older wireless printers can struggle to connect to modern routers. If yours won't join your network, a USB cable is often the simplest fix.

Check the maker's support site for your model before you buy cartridges. If there's no driver for your computer, that settles it.

When to give up and buy new

It's probably time to move on if:

  • A full set of cartridges costs as much as, or more than, a new printer.
  • Cartridges are hard to find, or only available from one or two shops.
  • There's no driver for your computer, and it won't work over AirPrint or Mopria.
  • An inkjet with a built-in print head still prints badly after a few days of cleaning and resting.

If you do replace it, see Inkjet or laser printer? and our cheapest printers to run. And don't just bin the old one: Recycling printer cartridges explains how to recycle it and its cartridges, and why you should reset it to factory settings first.

If it does come back to life, keep it that way by printing something at least once a week. See Printer maintenance.
